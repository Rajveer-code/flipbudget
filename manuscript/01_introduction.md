# Introduction

*Draft, Phase 7 of the autonomous execution pass. Grounded only in established results (analyses A and B, `METHODOLOGICAL_AUDIT.md` item 6); makes no claim about analysis C, which is pending the 458-row human audit.*

Benchmark leaderboards report accuracy as if it were observed. It is not. A model's reported score is the output of a fixed, deterministic verifier applied to free-text generations — a string comparison, a regex over a boxed answer, a symbolic equivalence check — and that verifier can be wrong. When it is, the number on the leaderboard is not the model's accuracy; it is the model's accuracy contaminated by the verifier's own error rate, in a direction and magnitude nobody has measured.

This project asks what a benchmark comparison is still entitled to claim once that contamination is taken seriously. We audit MATH-Hard's standard comparator on real model responses, find it disagrees with human judgment at a small but nonzero rate, and derive the set of true accuracy values consistent with what was actually observed — not a point estimate corrected by a guessed error rate, but a *partially identified set*, sharp under the weakest assumption the audit data will bear. The central empirical finding is not that the verifier is wrong often. It is that **how much a benchmark's verifier has been audited turns out to matter more than how good the benchmark's sample size is** — audit-estimation uncertainty, driven by how few human-labeled items exist to pin down the verifier's error rate, exceeds the field's already-reported sampling uncertainty for the majority of real model comparisons we can identify at all.

That finding immediately raises a design question the literature has not answered: if verifier auditing is the binding constraint, how should a fixed human-labeling budget be spent? We formalize this as a stratified-sampling allocation problem, derive the classical (Neyman, 1934) solution, and then show — on this project's own real, sparse audit data — that the classical formula's own assumption (a decent pilot estimate) fails in exactly the regime most benchmarks are actually audited in: when most audited strata have observed zero error events, naive plug-in allocation concentrates budget on whichever cell happened to draw a noisy nonzero rate, and is dominated by simply spreading the budget evenly. A stress-tested, sparse-data-aware allocation rule is the project's most directly usable output.

A third thread emerged from building an adversarial test suite for the verifier: MATH-Hard's evaluation harness computes accuracy two different ways from the same generations — one via LaTeX-aware symbolic parsing, one via locating the last `\boxed{}` span — and these two, both live, both in active use, disagree on 3.63% of real responses (95% CI [3.39%, 3.88%], full population, 17 models × 7 subjects). Every audit this project reports was conducted against the second of these; the field's reported accuracy comes from the first. We show this mismatch does not overturn the audit-estimation-dominance finding — tested directly, not assumed, including under a deliberately adversarial worst-case bound on the unaudited scorer's error rate — but it establishes a second, independent source of benchmark uncertainty: *which* automated criterion is used to score a benchmark is itself a measurable, sometimes consequential, choice.

**Contributions.** (1) A sharp partial-identification treatment of benchmark accuracy under an audited, imperfect verifier, adapting classical misclassification-bounds machinery (Horowitz & Manski, 1995; Molinari, 2008) to the benchmark-comparison setting — the adaptation, not the underlying mathematics, is the contribution. (2) An empirical demonstration, on real data from a widely-used benchmark, that audit-estimation uncertainty dominates sampling uncertainty for the majority of identifiable model comparisons. (3) A stratified audit-design methodology, including a documented failure mode of the textbook-optimal allocation rule under sparse real data and a corrected, tested alternative. (4) A secondary, honestly-scoped finding that automated-scorer choice is itself a measurable, non-dominant but non-negligible source of benchmark uncertainty, with a worst-case sensitivity bound rather than an assumed-safe headline.
