\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Benchmark validity and benchmark quality.}
A line of work assesses benchmarks as instruments rather than as leaderboards, asking whether
a benchmark measures what it claims and whether its construction supports the inferences drawn
from it \citep{reuel2024betterbench, salaudeen2025measurement}. That work is upstream of this
one. It asks whether the questions and the construct are right; we take the benchmark as given
and ask whether the ranking it publishes is identified by its own scoring pipeline. The two
concerns compound: a benchmark can be a valid instrument and still produce an unidentified
ranking, and the reverse.

\paragraph{Scoring and answer extraction.}
That evaluation results move with configuration is documented for prompt format and for
reproducibility of evaluation pipelines \citep{sclar2023quantifying, biderman2024lessons},
and several papers propose better extractors, including learned ones
\citep{yu2024xfinder}. Our claim is not that a better extractor is available.
It is that no extractor is neutral, that the choice is currently unreported, and that the
consequence for a ranking can be bounded rather than argued about. A better extractor changes
the cell structure; it does not remove the need to say what happens when extractors disagree.

\paragraph{Inference for rankings.}
Rank intervals and simultaneous inference for ordered comparisons are established, and we use
the standard machinery rather than proposing new estimators; the bounds themselves are
partial-identification statements in the sense of \citet{manski2003partial}, and the interval
construction follows the finite-population sampling literature
\citep{cochran1977sampling, kish1965survey, clopper1934use}. The difference in setting is that
the usual uncertainty is sampling uncertainty over items, with the score of each item treated
as observed. Here the score itself is a partially identified quantity, so the interval has two
sources, and the audit-driven one does not shrink with more items.

\paragraph{Using a cheap signal with a small amount of ground truth.}
Prediction-powered inference and two-phase sampling designs combine an abundant proxy with a
small validated sample, which is structurally what an audit does. The verifier is the proxy
and the human labels are the validation. What differs here is the target and the assumption.
The target is a set of pairwise orderings subject to a simultaneous guarantee rather than a
single mean, and the assumption that makes a few hundred labels sufficient is about
cross-model homogeneity within agreement cells, which is stated as a parameter and varied
rather than adopted.

\paragraph{Verification bias in diagnostic testing.}
The structure of the problem is old. When a reference test is applied to a subset selected by
the index test, naive accuracy is biased, and classical corrections adjust for it. The
agreement-stratified design here is the multi-rater version of that idea, with the labels
deliberately allocated to the cells where the raters disagree.

\paragraph{What this paper adds.}
No estimator here is new. The contribution is the combination: verifier provenance established
from the leaderboard's own records, agreement stratification across three verifiers, an
identification result that makes the pooling assumption a reported parameter with a breakdown
value per ordering, a design that allocates labels between certifying and testing that
assumption, and a released audit.
