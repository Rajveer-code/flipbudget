\section{Audit Design}
\label{sec:audit-design}

\paragraph{The formal problem.} Given qualifying models with audit strata
(credited, wrong) and a fixed additional labeling budget $B$, choose the
allocation minimizing total variance of the resulting stratified
estimators---the classical stratified-sampling problem
\citep{neyman1934stratified}, solved by $n_s \propto \sqrt{p_s(1-p_s)}$.

\paragraph{The failure mode, found and fixed.} On our real audit data, 15
of 17 qualifying models have exactly zero observed false-credit events.
Plug-in Neyman weights are therefore near-zero for almost every stratum and
concentrate the entire additional budget on the one or two cells with the
largest---and noisiest, since it comes from a tiny $n$---observed rate.
Swept as a budget curve on the real 120-pair audit-only layer
(Figure~\ref{fig:audit-budget}, Table~\ref{tab:audit-design}):
\textbf{naive Neyman allocation stalls at 74\% unresolved even at
$16\times$ the current budget, while proportional allocation reaches 22\%,
and a greedy search over the actual discrete objective reaches 40\% at only
$4\times$ budget.} This does not contradict Neyman's optimality
proof---variance-optimality-given-the-true-$p$ is a poor proxy for this
discrete objective when the planning estimate itself is this unreliable, a
real, quantified, previously-undocumented limitation.

\paragraph{A second, real bug found building the second audit round.} The
four preregistered joint-scorer cells for the 458-row expansion had
sampling fractions differing up to $6\times$ relative to population size.
Naively pooling raw counts across cells is biased: verified numerically at
$+444\%$ relative error in a realistic scenario. Corrected with a
Horvitz--Thompson / post-stratified estimator before any real label was
processed (full derivation: Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-ht}); 7 regression
tests added.

\paragraph{Negative controls (falsifiability).} The framework is not
structurally biased toward reporting more uncertainty regardless of input:
constructed scenarios with an adequately large audit correctly report
\emph{stable} (full table: Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-figures}). Notably,
even a \emph{perfect} scorer at the real audit's median size ($n=11$)
still shows $5.6\times$ dominance---the headline finding is a statement
about audit thinness, not necessarily large true scorer error, which the
audit-design contribution directly targets.
