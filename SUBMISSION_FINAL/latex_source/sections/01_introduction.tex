\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Benchmark leaderboards report accuracy as if it were observed. It is not. A
model's reported score is the output of a fixed, deterministic verifier
applied to free-text generations---a string comparison, a regex over a boxed
answer, a symbolic equivalence check---and that verifier can be wrong. When it
is, the number on the leaderboard is not the model's accuracy; it is the
model's accuracy contaminated by the verifier's own error rate, in a
direction and magnitude nobody has measured. Figure~\ref{fig:conceptual}
sketches the resulting measurement pipeline and where each source of
uncertainty enters it.

This paper asks what a benchmark comparison is still entitled to claim once
that contamination is taken seriously. We audit MATH-Hard's standard
comparator on real model responses, find it disagrees with human judgment at
a small but nonzero rate, and derive the set of true accuracy values
consistent with what was actually observed---not a point estimate corrected
by a guessed error rate, but a \emph{partially identified set}, sharp under
the weakest assumption the audit data will bear. The central empirical
finding is not that the verifier is wrong often. It is that \textbf{how much
a benchmark's verifier has been audited turns out to matter more than how
good the benchmark's sample size is}: audit-estimation uncertainty, driven by
how few human-labeled items exist to pin down the verifier's error rate,
exceeds the field's already-reported sampling uncertainty for the majority of
real model comparisons we can identify at all.

That finding immediately raises a design question the literature has not
answered: if verifier auditing is the binding constraint, how should a fixed
human-labeling budget be spent? We formalize this as a stratified-sampling
allocation problem, derive the classical \citep{neyman1934stratified}
solution, and then show---on this paper's own real, sparse audit data---that
the classical formula's own assumption (a decent pilot estimate) fails in
exactly the regime most benchmarks are actually audited in: when most audited
strata have observed zero error events, naive plug-in allocation concentrates
budget on whichever cell happened to draw a noisy nonzero rate, and is
dominated by simply spreading the budget evenly. A stress-tested,
sparse-data-aware allocation rule is this paper's most directly usable
output.

A third thread emerged from building an adversarial test suite for the
verifier: MATH-Hard's evaluation harness computes accuracy two different ways
from the same generations---one via LaTeX-aware symbolic parsing
(\exactmatch, via \code{math\_verify}), one via locating the last
\code{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} span (\scoreboxed)---and these two, both
live, both in active use, disagree on 3.63\% of real responses (95\% CI
[3.39\%, 3.88\%], full population, 17 models $\times$ 7 subjects). Every
human audit this paper reports was conducted against \scoreboxed; the field's
reported accuracy comes from \exactmatch. We show this mismatch does not
overturn the audit-estimation-dominance finding---tested directly, not
assumed, including under a deliberately adversarial worst-case bound on the
unaudited scorer's error rate---but it establishes a second, independent
source of benchmark uncertainty: \emph{which} automated criterion is used to
score a benchmark is itself a measurable, sometimes consequential, choice.
Independent, production-scale confirmation that this general problem is real
(not specific to our framing) is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related-work}.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1) A sharp partial-identification treatment of
benchmark accuracy under an audited, imperfect verifier, adapting classical
misclassification-bounds machinery
\citep{manski1995identification,molinari2008partial} to the
benchmark-comparison setting---the adaptation, not the underlying
mathematics, is the contribution. (2) An empirical demonstration, on real
data from a widely-used benchmark, that audit-estimation uncertainty
dominates sampling uncertainty for the majority of identifiable model
comparisons. (3) A stratified audit-design methodology
(Section~\ref{sec:audit-design}), including a documented failure mode of the
textbook-optimal allocation rule under sparse real data and a corrected,
tested alternative. (4) A secondary, honestly-scoped finding that
automated-scorer choice is itself a measurable, non-dominant but
non-negligible source of benchmark uncertainty, with a worst-case sensitivity
bound rather than an assumed-safe headline. Sections~\ref{sec:problem-formulation}--\ref{sec:theory}
develop the identification theory; Section~\ref{sec:audit-design} the
audit-design problem; Sections~\ref{sec:empirical-setup}--\ref{sec:results}
the empirical results, including the scorer-choice and worst-case analyses;
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} limitations.
